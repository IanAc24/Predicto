# Build the watchlist first, predict honestly

Build this as a watchlist-centric React app whose first deliverable is the interface running entirely on fixture data behind a frozen API contract, then add data ingestion, a FastAPI backend and a deliberately modest prediction pipeline behind that contract. The honest ceiling matters more than any technology choice: credible out-of-sample studies put next-day direction accuracy for liquid stocks at roughly **50-55%**, often indistinguishable from a coin flip or an "always up" baseline ([arXiv 1902.03125](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.03125), [arXiv 2108.10826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.10826)), and a few weeks of watching Nike and Adidas yields 12 to 60 predictions, far too few to tell skill from luck. The "weeks of observation" idea is still the right product, but for a different reason than intended: the model learns from years of history across many stocks, while the observation weeks build a timestamped live track record, which is the only leak-proof test of whether the model works. The recommended stack is Vite + React + TanStack Router/Query + Tailwind/shadcn + TradingView Lightweight Charts on the front, FastAPI + PostgreSQL + Polars/LightGBM on the back, in one repository using pnpm and uv, all of which run natively on Windows 11. For personal use the whole thing runs at **$0-30 per month** and sits outside US adviser regulation; showing prices or signals to other people changes both the data licence (from about $50 to $500 per month and up) and the legal position, so "personal or public" is the first decision to make. Adidas is the hard case in the brief: most of the free US-centric data sources surveyed do not cover XETRA, and non-US coverage was not verified for any provider, so it is flagged as a gap that one early spike must close. The roadmap below splits the work into 22 packages across four phases, with the seven interface packages able to run in parallel once the contract and fixtures exist.

## Six weeks of Nike and Adidas cannot distinguish skill from luck

The user's picture of the product is an app that studies followed stocks for weeks and then issues a good call. The statistics do not support the "then issues a good call" step if the weeks of observation are the evidence. The standard error of a hit rate near 50% is about 0.5 divided by the square root of the number of predictions. Six weeks of daily calls on two stocks is about 60 predictions, giving a standard error of roughly 6.5 percentage points, so a model with a true 53% hit rate will plausibly display anything from about 40% to 66%. With a weekly horizon the same six weeks gives 12 non-overlapping predictions and a standard error near 14 points. Nike and Adidas also move together as sector peers, so the effective sample is smaller still. Separating a 52% model from a coin at two standard errors needs on the order of **2,500 independent predictions**; even a full year of daily calls on two stocks (about 500) leaves a standard error above 2 points. This arithmetic is this report's own, built on the research notes' formula, not a published figure.

The published evidence explains why the true edge is that small. A deep-LSTM study on the S&P 500 found about 51% directional accuracy, not statistically significant ([arXiv 1902.03125](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.03125)). A 2025 study's headline 57% was the best of 35 trials whose average was **50.43%** ([arXiv 2512.15738](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.15738)). A 2026 preprint's 71% used the same day's open as an input and predicted open-to-close, not next-day, direction ([arXiv 2608.26106](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2608.26106.html)). Where machine learning does show an edge, it is cross-sectional: Gu, Kelly and Xiu's widely cited result is a long-short decile portfolio across thousands of stocks with an out-of-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.35, driven by momentum, liquidity and volatility signals ([NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf), [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Empirical-Asset-Pricing-via-Machine-Learning)), and that payoff falls by 62% once microcaps are excluded ([CUHK](https://cbk.bschool.cuhk.edu.hk/the-limitations-of-using-ai-to-pick-stocks/)). Published predictors lose 58% of their return after publication ([McLean & Pontiff](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)). Off-the-shelf time-series foundation models show gains over a random walk that are "small and sparse" on liquid US stocks ([arXiv 2606.27100](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27100)). Large, heavily covered names like Nike and Adidas are exactly where these edges are weakest.

### What the observation weeks are actually good for

The design that keeps the user's concept and stays honest separates learning from observing. The model trains on years of daily history pooled across a few hundred liquid stocks, because that is the only way to get enough data. When a stock joins the watchlist, the app enters an **observing state** for that stock: it records a timestamped shadow prediction every scoring day and later resolves each one against what happened. After the configured period (four to eight weeks), the app starts displaying predictions alongside that stock's own live record. The observation period therefore does not make the model smarter; it produces the one kind of evidence that cannot be contaminated by look-ahead, selection or backtest overfitting, which the research notes identify as the only fully leak-proof test.

Three presentation rules follow from the evidence. First, show a probability with its base rate, not a verdict: honest probabilities will cluster in a narrow band around 45-55%, and anything showing 80-90% confidence is miscalibrated by construction. Second, make "no signal" the default state and benchmark every number against an always-up model, because upward drift flatters raw accuracy ([arXiv 2108.10826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.10826)). Third, prefer a horizon of one to four weeks over daily calls, and consider a relative target; "probability Nike outperforms Adidas over the next month" matches how the evidence is structured, since the documented edge comes from ranking stocks against each other. Weekly-horizon evidence specifically is thin in the research gathered, so the horizon is a judgement call. Regulators also treat accuracy claims as a fraud marker: a September 2026 SEC complaint featured AI signals with a claimed "98% accuracy rate" ([InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/regulation-legal-compliance/fake-signals-frozen-accounts-sec-sues-crypto-ai-platform-over-125m/268436)), and the FTC found a product advertised as 98% accurate to be about 53% accurate ([Benesch](https://www.beneschlaw.com/insight/one-year-in-ftcs-operation-ai-comply-continues-under-new-administration-signaling-enduring-enforcement-focus/pdf/)).

### The Adidas problem

Adidas trades primarily on XETRA as ADS, with a US ADR under ADDYY; Nike is NYSE: NKE. The research did not verify XETRA or OTC-ADR coverage for any provider, and this is the most important data gap in the plan. What the notes do establish points in an unhelpful direction: Tiingo lists US and Chinese stocks, Alpaca's free feed is the US IEX venue, and Massive's plans are US stock tiers. EODHD's $19.99 "All-World" plan is the only surveyed product whose name implies global coverage, and that implication was not checked against XETRA. Whether IEX-sourced feeds carry an OTC-traded ADR such as ADDYY is also unverified. EDGAR's structured fundamentals serve US filers, so Adidas fundamentals need another source; none was identified. Licensing for displaying Deutsche Boerse prices was not researched at all.

The practical consequences shape the build even before the gap is closed. Instrument identifiers in the API contract must be exchange-qualified (for example `XNYS:NKE`, `XETR:ADS`) and carry currency and time zone, because the two stocks trade in different currencies, on different holiday calendars, and close at different times of day, so "data as of" is per exchange. A cross-listed comparison such as Nike versus Adidas must decide whether to compare in local currency or a common one. A one-day spike (work package 2.2) should test two routes: XETRA ADS through a global provider, or the USD-denominated ADR through a US provider, accepting thinner trading in exchange for one currency, one calendar and one vendor. yfinance is acceptable for that local experiment only; users have reported rate-limit blocks lasting more than 24 hours ([yfinance discussion](https://github.com/ranaroussi/yfinance/discussions/2431)).

### Licensing only bites when someone else sees the screen

Every low-cost plan surveyed is a personal or internal-use licence. Massive states that showing its data to anyone else in a web or mobile app requires a Business subscription regardless of volume ([Massive knowledge base](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/which-plan-do-i-need-to-show-massive-data-in-my-app)). Exchange fees stack on top for real-time display: IEX real-time TOPS is $500 per month, with a filed increase to $1,000 whose effective status is unconfirmed ([IEX fee schedule](https://iex.io/resources/trading/fee-schedule), [SEC notice](https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/iex/2026/34-105989.pdf)), while end-of-day usage is not fee-liable under the UTP plan ([UTP policies](https://www.utpplan.com/DOC/datapolicies.pdf)). No vendor was confirmed to publish a sub-$100 plan permitting public display of US equity prices. A daily-bar prediction app needs only end-of-day data, which is the cheapest tier everywhere, and a provider-abstraction layer built from day one lets a personal-licence source be swapped for a licensed feed without touching the app.

## A typed contract joins a React SPA to a Python backend

The stack follows from two facts: the user prefers React, and the prediction pipeline is Python regardless. A client-rendered **Vite + React SPA** with TanStack Router and TanStack Query is the lowest-burden frontend, because server rendering adds little to a personal dashboard and the charting library is client-only anyway ([Lightweight Charts docs](https://tradingview.github.io/lightweight-charts/docs)). TanStack Start is the upgrade path on the same router if server rendering is later wanted ([TanStack Start](https://tanstack.com/start/latest/docs/framework/react/overview)); Next.js is the better choice only if public, search-indexed pages become central. Tailwind with shadcn/ui is the recommended component layer; its current version was not verified from primary sources.

For charts, **TradingView Lightweight Charts v5.2.1** handles candlesticks, volume histograms and prediction markers, is Apache 2.0 with a required TradingView attribution, and now ships an agent skill in its repository to help AI coding assistants use it ([releases](https://github.com/tradingview/lightweight-charts/releases)). Pair it with Recharts for the calibration and hit-rate charts on the track-record screen. Highcharts Stock is more complete but costs $366 per seat for Core plus $366 for Stock ([Highcharts shop](https://shop.highcharts.com/)).

The backend is a single **FastAPI** service sharing one Python codebase with the pipeline. FastAPI emits OpenAPI 3.1 and its documentation recommends Hey API (`@hey-api/openapi-ts`) for generating a typed TypeScript client ([FastAPI docs](https://fastapi.tiangolo.com/advanced/generate-clients/)). Because the user wants to start with the interface, this plan inverts the usual order: a hand-authored OpenAPI 3.1 file is the first artefact, the TypeScript client and mock handlers are generated from it, and the FastAPI implementation is later tested for conformance against it. Mock Service Worker is the suggested mocking tool; it was not part of the research and is a suggestion from general practice. Pin exact versions of FastAPI, uv and Hey API, since all three are 0.x or "initial development" and ship breaking changes in minor releases ([FastAPI releases](https://github.com/fastapi/fastapi/releases), [Hey API](https://heyapi.dev/openapi-ts/get-started)). Hey API requires Node.js 22 or later.

Plain **PostgreSQL** is the system of record. A watchlist-scale price history is tens of megabytes, and even 5,000 tickers over 20 years is a low-single-digit number of gigabytes, so TimescaleDB and ClickHouse are unnecessary. Run Postgres in Docker locally or use Neon's free tier (1 GB per project, scale-to-zero after five minutes) ([Neon](https://neon.com/pricing)). Training data lives in Parquet files read with Polars or DuckDB, both of which ship current Windows-compatible releases ([Polars](https://pypi.org/project/polars/), [DuckDB](https://pypi.org/project/duckdb/)). Scheduling is a plain idempotent Python command triggered by Windows Task Scheduler for a personal build, or by a GitHub Actions schedule, which is best-effort and can drop runs under load ([GitHub docs](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/workflows-and-actions/events-that-trigger-workflows)). Authentication is unnecessary for a single-user local app; if the app goes public, Clerk (free to 50,000 monthly retained users) or Supabase Auth (free to 50,000 monthly active users) issue JWTs that FastAPI can verify ([Clerk](https://clerk.com/pricing), [Supabase](https://supabase.com/pricing)), and Railway at $5-20 per month hosts the API, cron and database together ([Railway](https://railway.com/pricing)).

On Windows 11, uv and pnpm install as native binaries; uv workspaces share one lockfile across the API and pipeline packages ([uv docs](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/concepts/projects/workspaces/)). Avoid Bash-only scripts and `make`, add a `.gitattributes` forcing LF line endings, and keep every command runnable from PowerShell and Linux CI alike. The repository layout is `apps/web` (React), `apps/api` (FastAPI), `packages/core` (shared Python models and vendor clients), `packages/pipeline` (ingest, features, training, scoring), `packages/api-client` (generated TypeScript) and `contracts/` (the OpenAPI file and fixtures).

## The pipeline scores nightly and writes to an immutable ledger

The architecture is batch, not online: inputs change once a day, so a nightly job writes predictions to a table and the API only reads them. The job runs five idempotent stages keyed by date: ingest bars and corporate actions and abort on failed quality checks; recompute features; load the production model, score and calibrate; resolve outcomes for earlier predictions; compute monitoring metrics. Three correctness decisions matter more than model choice. Store raw prices plus adjustment factors and derive adjusted series at read time, mirroring Qlib's `$factor` convention, because back-adjusted prices change with every new split or dividend ([Qlib docs](https://qlib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/component/data.html)). Stamp every datum with when it became knowable, not just when it occurred. Use a real exchange calendar; `exchange_calendars` and `pandas_market_calendars` are both current ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/exchange_calendars/), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pandas-market-calendars/)), though XETRA calendar support was not specifically verified.

Features are the unglamorous set the literature agrees on: multi-window returns and momentum, realised volatility, volume and liquidity measures, a handful of technical indicators, and market and sector context, each ranked or z-scored across the training universe per date as Qlib's cross-sectional processors do ([Qlib docs](https://qlib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/component/data.html)). Compute them with Polars and TA-Lib, which now ships Windows wheels ([TA-Lib](https://pypi.org/project/TA-Lib/)); avoid the original `pandas-ta`, whose repository returned a 404 and whose PyPI history is beta-only, in favour of `pandas-ta-classic` ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pandas-ta/), [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pandas-ta-classic/)). The label lives in its own versioned module; the recommended starting target is five-day forward return in excess of a benchmark, with a next-day-sign classifier trained alongside for comparison. Any horizon beyond one day creates overlapping labels and forces a purge gap in validation ([LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/label-definition-and-prediction-horizon/)).

The model ladder starts with naive baselines (always-up, yesterday's sign), then regularised logistic regression, then **LightGBM** with heavy regularisation, all CPU-only with Windows wheels ([scikit-learn](https://pypi.org/project/scikit-learn/), [LightGBM](https://pypi.org/project/lightgbm/)). Evaluation is walk-forward by date using scikit-learn's `TimeSeriesSplit` with its `gap` parameter as the purge, or skfolio's `WalkForward` ([sklearn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.TimeSeriesSplit.html), [skfolio](https://skfolio.org/generated/skfolio.model_selection.WalkForward.html)). Probabilities are calibrated on a disjoint recent block with `CalibratedClassifierCV`, using sigmoid unless there are well over a thousand calibration samples ([sklearn calibration](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)). MLflow with a local SQLite backend tracks runs and model versions at no cost ([MLflow](https://pypi.org/project/mlflow/)). If a backtesting library is used, mind the licences: backtesting.py is AGPL-3.0 and vectorbt carries a Commons Clause, while bt and skfolio are permissive ([backtesting.py](https://github.com/kernc/backtesting.py), [vectorbt](https://github.com/polakowo/vectorbt), [bt](https://pypi.org/project/bt/)).

Language models belong at the edges. They can turn news into structured sentiment features and narrate a prediction from its SHAP attributions, and cost is negligible for a watchlist: at Claude Haiku 5.5's listed $0.10 and $0.50 per million input and output tokens, with a 50% batch discount ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)), a few thousand tokens per stock per day is a fraction of a cent. The constraint is contamination, since models memorise prices and outcomes inside their training window, so only features generated live or on post-cutoff data count as clean evidence ([arXiv 2309.17322](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.17322), [arXiv 2504.14765](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)). The same caution applies to pretrained foundation models such as Chronos-2 or Kronos, which are optional late experiments used as extra features.

The piece specific to this product is the **prediction ledger and observation state machine**. Each instrument on the watchlist is in one of three states: observing (shadow predictions logged, countdown shown), active (predictions displayed with the live record), or no-signal (the calibrated probability is too close to the base rate to publish). Ledger rows are append-only and written before the outcome is knowable, with model version and feature hash. That table is simultaneously the product's main screen, its honest evaluation, and its substantiation file if a claim is ever challenged.

## Personal use sits outside adviser rules; publishing does not

This section is background research, not legal advice. For an app only its builder uses, the US Investment Advisers Act is not engaged because nobody else is being advised; only data-vendor terms apply. That changes the moment others use it. A public app relies on the publisher's exclusion construed in Lowe v. SEC, which protects impersonal, bona fide, regularly circulated publications that do not offer advice "attuned to any specific portfolio" ([Lowe v. SEC](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/472/181)); a federal court applied it to dismiss a claim against Seeking Alpha in August 2024 ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/8/no-need-for-seeking-alpha-to-seek-registration)). A watchlist that filters a uniform signal everyone sees is arguably still impersonal, but no source settled that line, and it is the question to put to a securities lawyer before any public launch. The design consequence is cheap to adopt now: the prediction engine never reads user-specific data, and the interface says "model estimate" and "probability" rather than "buy" or "sell".

Publisher status does not excuse misleading claims. The SEC's first "AI washing" settlements cost Delphia and Global Predictions $400,000 combined, partly over unsubstantiated performance claims ([O'Melveny](https://omm.com/insights/alerts-publications/sec-targets-ai-washing-in-charges-against-two-investment-advisers)), and the SEC sanctioned nine advisers in 2023 for advertising hypothetical performance to mass audiences ([Dinsmore](https://www.dinsmore.com/?p=32958)). The proposed predictive-analytics rule was withdrawn in June 2025 ([Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/sec-withdraws-14-pending-rule-proposals)), so exposure runs through antifraud and FTC deception standards, not a bespoke AI rule. Backtests must be labelled hypothetical and kept visually separate from the live record.

Because the user follows a German-listed stock, jurisdiction deserves an explicit decision. In the EU, the Market Abuse Regulation imposes identity, objectivity and conflict-disclosure duties on anyone producing public investment recommendations, with fines up to EUR 5 million for individuals ([ESMA](https://www.esma.europa.eu/press-news/esma-news/esma-addresses-investment-recommendations-made-social-media-platforms), [PwC Legal](https://legal.pwc.de/en/news/articles/eu-reiterates-rules-to-regulating-finfluencers)). The UK treats a recommendation on a named share as within its advice perimeter ([FCA PERG 8.26](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/8/26.html)). These bite on publication to others, not on private use, but they make a public launch materially harder outside the US. On app stores, Apple's guidelines say investing apps should come from the financial institution providing the service and that regulated financial services apps should be submitted by a legal entity, not an individual ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)); a web app avoids the gatekeeping but not the underlying law.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the product. The valuable asset is not a clever model, since the literature leaves little room for one on two large sportswear stocks; it is the ledger: a growing, immutable, timestamped record of what the app said and what happened. Starting with the interface turns out to serve that well, because designing the prediction panel and track-record screen first forces every later package to produce exactly the fields that honest presentation needs, including sample sizes, base rates and the three states. A plausible outcome after several months is a track record showing the model does not beat the always-up baseline on these two stocks. The plan treats that as a legitimate result the interface can display, not as a failure to be tuned away, and the trial log in package 3.2 exists to make tuning-until-it-works visible.

Two unknowns can change the plan and should be resolved early. The Adidas data-coverage spike decides the vendor and whether the comparison runs in one currency or two. The audience decision determines whether this stays a $0-30 per month personal tool or becomes a product needing a display licence, a lawyer's view on the watchlist-as-filter question and, outside the US, compliance with recommendation rules that have no small-developer exemption in the sources reviewed.
